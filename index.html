Exploratory graphs

1 · Richer countries emit more CO₂ per person

GDP per capita vs CO₂ per capita, 2018. Log-log axes; bubble area = population.

What we noticed: There is a strong positive relationship between GDP per capita and CO₂ emissions per person across the 183 countries shown in 2018 — a log-transformed correlation of 0.88. North American countries sit toward the higher end on both measures, while Sub-Saharan African countries sit toward the lower end. Countries with similar GDP per capita can still have vastly different emissions (e.g. India vs. Nigeria, or China vs. the Dominican Republic), suggesting income alone doesn't tell the whole story. The largest-population countries also aren't necessarily the highest per-person emitters — population size and per-capita emissions are fairly distinct.

2 · Per-capita emissions by income group, 1960-2018

Median CO₂ per capita across countries in each income group (2021 World Bank classification applied to every year). Hover for values.

What we noticed: High-income countries have the highest median CO₂ per capita in every year shown. That bracket started at 3.54 tonnes in 1960, held relatively steady around 8 tonnes from 1975-2002, peaked at 9.07 tonnes in 2004, then dropped to 6.67 tonnes by 2018 — well above every other income category throughout. Upper-middle-income countries rose from 0.65 to 3.22 tonnes, lower-middle-income countries from 0.22 to 0.90 tonnes, and low-income countries from 0.05 to 0.17 tonnes. Every income class increased its median CO₂ per capita from 1960 to 2018.

3 · Who are the biggest emitters? Depends how you count

Top 15 countries in 2018.

What we noticed: There's very little overlap between the two rankings for 2018. By CO₂ per capita, Qatar (32.4 t), Kuwait (21.6 t), and the United Arab Emirates (20.8 t) top the list. By total CO₂ emissions, China (10,313 Mt), the United States (4,981 Mt), and India (2,435 Mt) top the list. Which countries look like the "biggest emitters" depends entirely on how you choose to measure output.

4 · Wealth vs life expectancy

GDP per capita (log) vs life expectancy at birth, 2018. Life expectancy is the mean of the male and female series.

What we noticed: There is a strong positive relationship between GDP per capita (log) and life expectancy at birth. Lower-income countries cluster around 55-70 years, while higher-income countries cluster around 80-85 years. Sub-Saharan African countries dominate the lower-left of the chart (lower GDP, lower life expectancy), while European and Central Asian countries dominate the upper-right (higher GDP, higher life expectancy). The spread of life expectancy narrows considerably at higher incomes — meaning larger gains in GDP per capita correspond to smaller gains in longevity once a country is already wealthy.

5 · Paths through wealth-carbon space

Each line traces one country from 1960 to 2018; larger dots mark each decade. Hover a line to isolate it.

What we noticed: The United States, United Kingdom, and Germany all show rising GDP per capita with CO₂ emissions per person declining in later years. China and India, by contrast, show increases in both measures from 1960 to 2018. Brazil follows a similar upward trend, though its emissions per person stay relatively low compared with the U.S., U.K., and Germany. China's trail spans the greatest distance on the chart, reflecting the largest proportional changes in both GDP per capita and CO₂ emissions per person over this period.

6 · U.S. vs China: depends how you count

CO₂ per person (left) and total CO₂ (right), 1960-2018. Total converted from kilotonnes to billion tonnes. Hover for values.

What we noticed: The emissions for China and the United States can be characterized differently based on if you look at total or per capita emissions. Looking at totals, China far exceeds the United States post-2005, and if you look at per-capita the reverse is true. Focusing on just two countries may be limiting as a visualization but the interpretation is very simple.

Data & transformations

Source: World Bank World Development Indicators, via light-and-salt/World-Bank-Data-by-Indicators. scripts/prepare_data.py builds data/wb_clean.csv:

  • Joins GDP per capita (constant 2010 US$), CO₂ per capita and total, population, life expectancy, energy use, renewable share and urban share by country and year.
  • Keeps only countries; regional and income aggregates (“World”, “High income”, …) are dropped.
  • Life expectancy is the average of the female and male series (no total series in the source).
  • CO₂ data ends in 2018, so charts stop there.

Write-up

Design rationale

The question

We wanted to know whether countries emit more carbon per-capita as they get richer per-capita and, if so, whether that relationship has evolved since 1960. Our data exploration pointed in two directions. Across all countries wealth and per-capita emissions are tightly correlated with a log-log correlation of 0.88 in 2018. But individual countries' paths showed different trends over time, and as a whole the log-log relationship between GDP and emissions evolved from a linear trend in the 90's to a curved relationship in 2010's (meaning wealthy countries have decoupled some economic growth from emissions). A static representation does not capture these changes over time, so we built an interactive plot that moves through time.

Visual encodings

  • GDP per-capita on x-axis and emissions per-capita on y-axis. This decision was straightforward as both axes represent the two parts of the relationship we are examining in our original question.
  • Log scales. GDP and CO₂ both span orders of magnitudes ($130 to $141k GDP per person and 0.01t to 62t of emissions per person). On linear axes the countries were squashed into the bottom left-corner with a few rich or high emitting countries taking up the rest of the plot. On a log-axes we can see trends across all income levels.
  • Fixed axes across all years. If the axes rescaled each year then countries may appear to move when the reality is that the frame changed. To avoid deceiving the audience, we fixed both axes to show only real change over time.
  • Bubble area for population. Including population is for context, not the main point, so we gave it a weaker encoding. We used a square-root scale so area, not radius, is proportional to population. This is to prevent large countries looking disproportionately larger. We also draw large countries first so smaller countries don't get lost behind them. We considered sizing the bubbles by total carbon emissions, but that would have muddled the per-capita data story we were pursuing so for clarity we chose population.
  • Coloring for region. Region is a categorical attribute, so each region gets a distinct color that they keep over the years. Seven regions is near the limit of hues people can tell apart, so we added the ability to highlight specific regions. We added this interaction so that the audience can explore region to region similarities. We could have colored by another attribute like total GDP, life expectancy, etc. but we thought region may better address the primary question.

Interaction techniques

  • Year slider with animation. The animation shows the drift of our cloud of points over time, and the slider lets the reader stop on any year. We considered showing change at fixed instances like decades, but for finer granularity we chose continuous animation, which lets the audience stop on any snapshot. We also added the ability to show trails (expanded below) to cover the main weakness of a continuous animation; that it's hard to remember the changes over time.
  • Trails and "Find". Clicking a bubble, or picking a country in "Find", draws that country's path up to the selected year. Users can add additional trails by searching for more countries with "Find". "Find" lets readers locate a specific country among ~200.
  • Highlight by region or income group. The highlight dropdown, or a click on the legend, fades the other groups so that the audience can isolate a subset of countries while still getting the surrounding context.
  • Optional trend line. We have the option of imposing a LOESS curve fitted to each year's countries, in the same log space the chart is drawn in. We considered a straight regression line, but the data in the 2000's appeared to gain a bend that flattens among high GDP countries. LOESS fits small local lines so the trend is dynamic, although in early years the line is vulnerable to some outlier points. Each country counts equally, instead of weighing by population, because we're interested in country to country comparisons.
  • Tooltips, zoom/pan and search. Hovering shows exact values in a tooltip, while zoom/pan help with the dense clusters of countries where bubbles may overlap.

Data transformations

  • Kept only individual countries. Filtered out World Bank aggregates such as "World" and "High income" to avoid mistakenly plotting non-countries.
  • Used GDP per capita in constant 2010 USD. A purchasing power variable would have been better for comparison, but due to our visual starting in the 1960's this variable was not available for our full time span.
  • Log transformation of both GDP and emissions was appropriate due to the many magnitudes of data both attributes cover ($130 to $141k GDP per person and 0.01t to 62t of emissions per person).
  • Countries missing a value in a year are hidden that year, so the number of countries shown grows from about 100 in the 1960's to about 185 in 2018. Additionally, emissions data ends in 2018 so the timeline stops there.

What we considered and dropped

  • A geographic map showing emissions over time. A map would have been more engaging and intuitive, but it struggles to show the relationship between GDP and emissions. Additionally, large countries would have dominated visually.
  • Isolating just the U.S. and China Emissions from the U.S. and China dominate media coverage domestically, so we briefly considered focusing on just those two countries. The scope felt too limiting and could still be achieved in a well designed visual for all countries so we opted for the latter.
  • Bringing in life-expectancy We considered bringing in a third dimension like life expectancy, but the chart quickly became confusing and we could only effectively showcase one x-y comparison on the axes effectively.

Development process

How we split the work.

  • Aidan: Revisions on exploratory graphs, contributions to primary interactive plot interactivity (initial trails, improvement to searchability), dataset/question ideating, and contributions to final write-up.
  • Anirudh: Exploratory graph 6, contributions to primary plot interactivity (improvement to trails, improvement to searchability, refining highlights), dataset/question ideating, and contributions to final write-up.
  • Jack: Exploratory graph first drafts, primary plot first draft, dataset/question ideating, and contributions to final write-up.

Time spent. Roughly 18 people-hours in total: about 3 on data exploration and cleaning, 4 on the exploratory graphs and its revisions, 6 on the main interactive graphic and its revisions, and 5 on styling, testing and the write-up.

What took the most time.

  • Data wrangling and cleaning. We had familiarity with dataset from project 2 but considering our choices of variables and how we're going to process the data to feed to D3 was more difficult than expected.
  • Getting the animation right. Making sure that bubbles don't hide each other, that movement is trackable, that the animation is responsive to the user, ease of country selection, and all the other aspects we detailed above was a long process of revisions.

With more time we would have explored connecting other similar plots showcasing life expectancy, energy use, and other interesting metrics so users could explore changes over time across multiple metrics to find interesting patterns. For example, does a country that increases emissions over time also increase their life expectancy? Or are those two ideas decoupled?
